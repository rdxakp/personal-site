State of the art, out of reach

22 Oct 2026 4 min read Access

The best generative models are extraordinary — and getting cheaper by the month. The harder question is who can actually reach them. Right now, it isn't the people who would gain the most.

What state of the art costs

The four strongest models in the world are closed; the next four are open-weight, downloadable by anyone. The best open model trails the closed leader by a few index points — roughly four months of progress — at around 30% of the input price. Closed models cost about six times more to run, and switching the same workload can cut spend by over 70%.

At the cheap end it stops being abstract. A small open model answers for about $0.14 per million input tokens; the top closed model lists at $10. Open weights are the hedge against the meter — and for anyone building for people with no budget at all, the meter is the whole problem.

Who is actually excluded

Cheap isn't free, and free isn't reachable. Around 2.9 billion people are offline, and 96% of them live in low- and middle-income countries. In high-income countries about 94% of people use the internet; in low-income countries, about 23%. Africa holds 18% of the world's population and under 1% of global data centre capacity.

Then language. Of roughly 7,000 languages, only about 1,500 have enough digital material to build on. Models that work for the 1.5 billion English speakers degrade sharply for everyone else — and where they do function, they can cost up to five times more, run slower, and resist safety testing.

And the constraints stack: a shared phone, intermittent power, prepaid data, low literacy, no laptop. A frontier model behind a credit card isn't a tool for that person. It's a rumour.

Why it isn't only pricing

Drop the price of inference to zero and you still don't reach someone with no connection, no electricity and no device. The gap is a stack — connectivity, power, devices, language, skills, money — and each layer can exclude on its own. Capability stopped being the barrier a while ago; what remains is investment and intent.

There's a quieter problem too. If the data of the poorest is collected, shipped out and sold back to them, then access without ownership is just a new dependency.

How might we bridge the gap

Open weights as public infrastructure. A downloadable model runs at the cost of electricity, adapts locally, and no pricing decision can switch it off.

Small and on-device. Models under a billion parameters take 83% of all downloads, because they're the only ones that run on hardware people actually own. Lightweight translation models now run fully on-device, with no cloud round trip.

Voice-first, for the cheapest phone. Where typing and literacy are barriers, speech isn't. Voice assistants running on ordinary feature phones have already answered millions of questions in pilot programmes.

Language data as a locally-governed commons. Community-run voice collection, open licences that let communities set the terms, corpora built with local universities. Slower than scraping — and the only version that isn't extraction.

Measure in-language. You can't make a system safe in a language you can't test it in. Benchmarks and safety evaluations for low-resource languages are the precondition for using these tools in health, law and public services.

Fund the last mile. Connectivity, power and cheap devices are the unglamorous part and the biggest. Closing the digital divide by 2030 is estimated in the trillions, with the large majority of the benefit landing in developing countries. No model progress substitutes for a signal and a charged battery.

What I take from it

The frontier is racing toward capability. Almost nobody is racing toward the floor. One question asks how smart the best model is; the other asks how many people can use a good-enough one. The second decides whether any of this reaches the poorest billion — and it's mostly an engineering and governance problem, not a research one.

Drawn from published work by ITU, UNDP's Every Language Matters, Epoch AI, Artificial Analysis, MIT Sloan, the Gates Foundation language coalition, Microsoft's AI for Good Lab and Google's language-data partnerships.
